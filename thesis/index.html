Uncertainty Quantified Process-Property Maps for Ti-6Al-4V and Inconel 718

Laser powder bed fusion builds metal parts from powder, one thin layer at a time. How strong and dense a part comes out depends on how the machine is set up. My thesis builds maps that predict a part's properties from those settings, and says how sure each prediction is.

01

The process

A laser melts a thin layer of metal powder along a pattern of closely spaced lines. A blade then spreads fresh powder, and the laser melts the next slice on top. A few thousand layers make a part.

Five settings control how each layer melts:

P
Laser power. How much energy the beam delivers.
v
Scan speed. How fast the laser spot moves.
h
Hatch spacing. The gap between neighbouring lines.
t
Layer thickness. The depth of each powder layer.
d
Spot size. The width of the beam where it meets the powder.
02

The problem

With too little energy, the powder doesn't fully melt and gaps are left between tracks. This is lack of fusion. With too much, the melt pool becomes a deep, unstable keyhole that traps gas as it collapses.

Between the two is a window where parts come out dense and strong. Qualifying a material means finding that window, then showing that parts made inside it meet their specification. Doing that by trial and error takes many builds, and each build costs time, powder and machine hours.

03

One map for two alloys

Ti-6Al-4V and Inconel 718 melt at different temperatures and conduct heat differently, so the same machine settings give different results in each. A map drawn in machine settings only works for one alloy on one machine.

I combine the five settings with each alloy's thermal properties into four dimensionless groups. Each one compares two physical effects, so its value means the same thing whichever alloy is in the machine.

ΠH*
Normalised enthalpy. The energy the powder absorbs, compared with the energy needed to melt it.
Pe*
Péclet number. How fast the laser moves, compared with how fast heat spreads.
φ
Track overlap. How much each new track overlaps the one beside it.
λ
Layer margin. How far the beam reaches past one layer into the one below.
Technical detail

The groups use the spot diameter d as the length scale:

ΠH* = ηPρ Hm √(αm v d3)

Pe* = v dαm

φ = d − hd

λ = d − td

Here ρ is density, αm is thermal diffusivity at the melting point, and Hm = cp(Tm − T0) + L is the energy per unit mass to heat the powder from the plate temperature and melt it.

The absorptivity η is the fraction of laser power the powder takes in. I use a matched value of 0.60 for both alloys, so differences between them come from their thermal properties, not from an assumed difference in absorption.

04

Five properties, one model

For each build I measure five properties:

  • Relative density
  • Hardness
  • Surface roughness
  • Tensile properties
  • Charpy impact toughness

These properties are linked. A porous part is usually also softer, rougher and weaker. A multi-output Gaussian process models all five together, so a test of one property also tells the model something about the others.

For every setting, the model gives a prediction and a confidence band. The band is narrow near tests and widens where there is little data.

Technical detail

A Gaussian process treats an unknown function as a distribution over smooth curves. Once conditioned on test results, it gives a mean and a variance at every input. The variance is what turns a property map into an uncertainty quantified one.

The multi-output model shares one input kernel across all properties and adds a coregionalisation matrix that holds how strongly each pair of properties is correlated. The model learns the correlations from the data, so a well-measured property can inform a sparse one.

The inputs are the dimensionless groups from section 03. The demo above uses one input, two outputs, a squared-exponential kernel and fixed hyperparameters, so it runs in the browser.

05

Maps with confidence

A conventional process map shows where parts are predicted to be dense. An uncertainty quantified map also shows how sure that prediction is. That splits the map into three kinds of region: settings known to work, settings known to fail, and settings that need more tests.

For qualification, this means a datasheet can give a process window with a stated confidence, and the next builds can go where they reduce uncertainty the most.

Technical detail

For a specification limit ys, the probability that a part meets it is Φ((μ − ys) / σ), where μ and σ are the model's mean and standard deviation at that setting and Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution.

The solid line in the confidence view encloses settings where that probability is at least 95%. Wide grey bands around it show where more tests would sharpen the window.
